\begin{abstract}

\noindent In the current era of partisan polarization, partisanship strongly colors partisans' worldviews. But does it cause partisans to \textit{see} different things? In one experiment, respondents count errors in a short passage; in another, they count poorly parked cars in a photograph. We vary the party attributed to the author or drivers and replicate the writing experiment on Lucid. Pooling the experiments yields an effect of \PooledExperimentsAllpartisansEstimate{} standard deviations (SE = \PooledExperimentsAllpartisansSe{}) toward more favorable judgments of one's own party. The clearest individual effect is in parking: Democratic respondents count \CCESParkingDemocratDifference{} more poorly parked cars under the out-party cue (SE = \CCESParkingDemocratDifferenceSe{}); the corresponding difference among Republican respondents is \CCESParkingRepublicanDifference{} (SE = \CCESParkingRepublicanDifferenceSe{}). Writing evidence is weaker and sensitive to extreme responses in Lucid. In a separate MTurk survey, Republicans count more masked people than Democrats in a video of Trump supporters when extreme responses are capped. The experiments suggest a modest average partisan bias that varies across tasks; the mask-count evidence is less stable.
\end{abstract}

\newpage

\doublespacing

Partisans are increasingly polarized \citep{IyengarSoodLelkes2012}, with partisan cleavages outstripping some of the longer-standing racial cleavages \citep{IyengarWestwood2015}. In this paper, we explore whether polarization affects how partisans ``see.'' We test the hypothesis with simple visual evaluative tasks. In particular, we field two survey experiments and a survey. Uncapped writing estimates favor the respondent's own party, but the Lucid Republican estimate is sensitive to extreme responses. The pooled experimental evidence points toward a modest partisan bias, with the clearest individual effect on Democrats' counts of poorly parked cars.


Group loyalties can shape how people judge the same scene. In the classic study of a contentious football game, Princeton and Dartmouth students differed in the infractions they attributed to the teams \citep{HastorfCantril1954}. In a political setting, changing the purported cause of a protest changed judgments of whether the same demonstrators were obstructing or threatening pedestrians \citep{KahanEtAl2012}. Related experiments find that motivations influence interpretations of ambiguous visual stimuli \citep{BalcetisDunning2006} and that political agreement affects which photographs people regard as representative of a candidate's skin tone \citep{CarusoEtAl2009}.

These findings fit a broader literature on motivated political reasoning. People evaluate congenial arguments more favorably and scrutinize contrary arguments more closely \citep{TaberLodge2006}. Party identification can organize attention, memory, and evaluation around defending one's political group \citep{VanBavelPereira2018}. A meta-analysis of experiments that hold information constant while varying its political implications finds bias among both liberals and conservatives \citep{DittoEtAl2019}.

But partisan differences in survey answers need not be large or uniform. Accuracy incentives reduce partisan gaps in answers to factual questions \citep{BullockEtAl2015,PriorSoodKhanna2015}, and vague response options can inflate their size \citep{RoushSood2023}. Even a straightforward comparison of inauguration photographs can elicit answers favoring a respondent's preferred candidate \citep{SchaffnerLuks2018}. Our experiments ask whether party labels affect how many errors people find in a passage or how many cars they judge to be poorly parked. Respondents assess the material in front of them and give a count, rather than recall a political fact or choose among verbal descriptions of it.

\section{Data and Research Design}
To assess how partisans \textit{see}, we fielded two survey experiments with respondents recruited by YouGov as part of a Cooperative Congressional Election Study (CCES) module. In the first experiment, we presented people with a short passage and asked them to count the number of errors in it. We manipulated the perceived party of the person writing the text (see Figure~\ref{fig:mistakes_dem}). In the second experiment, we showed people a photo of a parking lot and asked them to estimate the number of poorly parked cars. We manipulated the perceived party of the people who parked the cars by changing the caption indicating where the photo was taken (see Figure~\ref{fig:parking_treat_dem}). We replicated the first experiment with a convenience sample recruited through Lucid, using a shorter passage. A validation study found that a Lucid sample came closer to national demographic benchmarks than an MTurk sample and reproduced several established experimental effects \citep{CoppockMcClellan2019}.

We complemented the survey experiments with a partisan evaluative task in a survey. In a survey conducted on MTurk, we asked respondents to watch a short video showing people wearing Trump clothing and estimate how many people in the video were wearing masks. In particular, our directions were as follows: ``Please watch the following short (10-second) video. You will be asked a question about it on the next screen. \ldots{} How many people in the video were wearing masks?''


We analyze the unmatched CCES sample without survey weights and report results from the matched, weighted sample in the replication materials. The writing task appeared before the election, and the parking task afterward. The main results focus on Democrats and Republicans, including leaners. The appendix reports descriptive results for all groups, including independents. For Lucid, we exclude previews and retain respondents who consented and passed the attention check. For MTurk, we pool the two survey waves and retain respondents who said they gave humorous or insincere answers never or rarely. The original instruments, party coding, sample counts, and sensitivity analyses are included in the replication materials.

Figure~\ref{fig:experiments} presents scaled means alongside standardized out-party minus in-party differences (Hedges' $g$, the small-sample-corrected version of Cohen's $d$). The pooled estimate combines the six study-by-party contrasts from the writing and parking experiments. We estimate their covariance by bootstrapping respondents within study and party, keeping each CCES respondent's writing and parking outcomes together. This accounts for clustering at the respondent level. The syntheses and the tests of moderation by partisan strength are exploratory; the appendix describes their construction. All intervals are 95\% confidence intervals. We examine uncapped and capped responses, the influence of individual observations, and differences across the two MTurk waves. The CCES analysis description on OSF was registered in November 2021, after the 2020 fieldwork (\url{https://osf.io/rqj8u}).

\section{Results}

Across the writing and parking experiments, the pooled out-party effect is \PooledExperimentsAllpartisansEstimate{} standard deviations (SE = \PooledExperimentsAllpartisansSe{}; 95\% CI: \PooledExperimentsAllpartisansLower{} to \PooledExperimentsAllpartisansUpper{}). This is a modest shift relative to the variation in counts within each task. The pooled estimate summarizes these experiments; the individual contrasts show where the evidence is strongest. Capping all counts at 20 yields a similar pooled effect of \CappedPooledEstimate{} standard deviations (SE = \CappedPooledSe{}; 95\% CI: \CappedPooledLower{} to \CappedPooledUpper{}). Figure~\ref{fig:experiments} shows both specifications; caps of 10 and 15 also preserve the pooled direction (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pooled_sensitivity}).

Across both writing studies, Democrats and Republicans find more errors when the text is attributed to the opposing party (Figure~\ref{fig:experiments}). These uncapped estimates all point toward partisan bias, although each confidence interval includes zero. The Lucid Republican estimate is sensitive to the right tail of the response distribution.

As Appendix Table~\ref{tab:error_sum_cces} shows, Democrats find \CCESWritingDemocratDemocratMean{} mistakes when they think the text is written by a Democrat compared to \CCESWritingDemocratRepublicanMean{} mistakes when they think the text is written by a Republican. On the other hand, Republicans find \CCESWritingRepublicanDemocratMean{} mistakes on average when they think the text is written by a Democrat and \CCESWritingRepublicanRepublicanMean{} mistakes when they think it is written by a Republican. The estimated differences are small. The point is especially clear when you look at the medians, which are the same within each party. The confidence intervals for the differences include zero, but also leave room for larger effects (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:effects}).


Appendix Table~\ref{tab:error_sum_lucid} presents the results of the replication on Lucid. Democrats find \LucidWritingDemocratDemocratMean{} (\LucidWritingDemocratDemocratSe{}) errors on average when they think Democrats wrote the text vs. \LucidWritingDemocratRepublicanMean{} (\LucidWritingDemocratRepublicanSe{}) when they think Republicans wrote it. On the Republican side, the corresponding numbers are \LucidWritingRepublicanDemocratMean{} (\LucidWritingRepublicanDemocratSe{}) (when they think Democrats wrote the text) vs. \LucidWritingRepublicanRepublicanMean{} (\LucidWritingRepublicanRepublicanSe{}) (when they think Republicans wrote it). Standard errors are in parentheses. These differences are imprecisely estimated (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:effects}). Lucid includes \LucidHundredCount{} responses of 100 errors, while the group medians are four or five. Capping counts at 20 changes the Republican contrast from \LucidWritingRepublicanDifference{} errors (SE = \LucidWritingRepublicanDifferenceSe{}) to \CappedLucidRepublicanEstimate{} (SE = \CappedLucidRepublicanSe{}); the Democratic contrast changes from \LucidWritingDemocratDifference{} (SE = \LucidWritingDemocratDifferenceSe{}) to \CappedLucidDemocratEstimate{} (SE = \CappedLucidDemocratSe{}). Caps of 10 and 15 likewise leave the Republican contrast near zero or slightly negative (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:response_caps}).

Pooling the four writing contrasts in their original units yields \PooledWritingAllpartisansEstimate{} additional errors under an out-party cue (SE = \PooledWritingAllpartisansSe{}; 95\% CI: \PooledWritingAllpartisansLower{} to \PooledWritingAllpartisansUpper{}). For context, the in-party means range from \WritingOwnMinimum{} to \WritingOwnMaximum{} errors. CCES supplies \PooledWritingCCESWeight{}\% of the inverse-variance weight. The pooled estimate is positive, but its interval still includes zero (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pooled_writing}).


We see a larger partisan bias in the experiment that manipulates the perceived party of the people who parked the cars. Democrats on average believe there are \CCESParkingDemocratDemocratMean{} poorly parked cars outside the Democratic Party meeting while they think there are \CCESParkingDemocratRepublicanMean{} poorly parked cars in front of the Republican Party meeting (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:parking_sum_cces}). As above, the medians are much closer at \CCESParkingDemocratDemocratMedian{} and \CCESParkingDemocratRepublicanMedian{} for the Democratic Party meeting and the Republican Party meeting, respectively. The difference is \CCESParkingDemocratDifference{} cars (SE = \CCESParkingDemocratDifferenceSe{}) and remains positive when any single respondent is omitted. Switching to Republicans, the gap is much narrower---Republicans on average think that there are \CCESParkingRepublicanDemocratMean{} poorly parked cars in front of the Democratic Party meeting and \CCESParkingRepublicanRepublicanMean{} in front of the Republican Party meeting. The mean difference is \CCESParkingRepublicanDifference{} cars (SE = \CCESParkingRepublicanDifferenceSe{}); the gap in medians is one.


We also ask whether the effects are larger among strong partisans than among weak partisans and leaners. The estimated difference between these effects is \StrengthCCESWritingEstimate{} errors in CCES writing (SE = \StrengthCCESWritingSe{}), compared with \StrengthLucidWritingEstimate{} in Lucid writing (SE = \StrengthLucidWritingSe{}). In parking, the difference is \StrengthCCESParkingEstimate{} cars (SE = \StrengthCCESParkingSe{}). These interaction estimates do not provide consistent evidence of larger effects among strong partisans (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:strength}).

Turning to the results from the MTurk survey, Republicans count more people wearing masks than Democrats (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:trump_sum}). We winsorized the responses at 15 because of extreme responses. Democrats on average spot \MasksDemocratMean{} (\MasksDemocratSe{}) masks and Republicans spot \MasksRepublicanMean{} (\MasksRepublicanSe{}) masks. The difference is \MasksDifference{} people (SE = \MasksDifferenceSe{}), with a 95\% confidence interval from \MasksLower{} to \MasksUpper{}. The medians are \MasksDemocratMedian{} for Democrats and \MasksRepublicanMedian{} for Republicans. Republicans count more masked people in both survey waves and when responses are capped at 10 or 20 (Figure~\ref{fig:masks}). Without a cap, reports as high as \MaskMaximum{} dominate the averages and the estimated difference reverses sign.

The higher Republican counts indicate that Republicans report more mask-wearing among Trump supporters. This is consistent with a more favorable assessment of one's own side if mask-wearing is judged favorably; the survey did not measure that judgment. Unlike the text and parking experiments, this comparison does not randomly assign party cues. We therefore present it separately from the pooled experimental effect.

\section{Evidence synthesis and partisan strength}

The writing synthesis uses inverse-variance weights to combine the four independent study-by-party mean differences in error counts. It assumes those differences are sufficiently comparable across the two passages to summarize in a common unit. We show the overall summary and summaries within respondent party (Table~\ref{tab:pooled_writing}).

To combine writing with parking, we divide each out-party minus in-party difference by its pooled within-condition standard deviation and apply Hedges' small-sample correction. We use generalized least squares to summarize the standardized effects (Table~\ref{tab:pooled_experiments}). The sampling covariance comes from 5,000 bootstrap draws of respondents within study and respondent party. Each sampled CCES respondent contributes all available outcomes, preserving dependence between the writing and parking tasks. Covariances between independent study-by-party samples are set to zero. The scaled means in Figure~\ref{fig:experiments} use the same standard deviations and Hedges corrections as the effects. Both cue means use the same synthesis weights, so their pooled difference equals the pooled Hedges' $g$. The scaled means use percentile bootstrap intervals to accommodate their skewed sampling distributions; the difference intervals use the normal approximation with the bootstrap covariance. The resulting estimate summarizes the observed experimental contrasts, rather than a random sample of possible tasks. The MTurk comparison is not included.

Strong partisans are CCES respondents coded 1 or 7 on \texttt{pid7} and Lucid respondents coded 1 or 10 on the provider's party-identification measure. Other partisans include weak identifiers and leaners. For each experiment, we fit a model fully interacting out-party assignment, strong identification, and Republican identification. We contrast strong and other partisans' treatment effects, giving Democrats and Republicans equal weight. Each experiment contributes one outcome per respondent; these interaction intervals use HC3 standard errors. Holm adjustment covers the three interaction tests. Table~\ref{tab:strength} reports both subgroup effects and their difference, avoiding comparisons based on whether one subgroup's interval happens to exclude zero.

\section{Sensitivity to extreme responses}

We repeat the experimental analysis with every count above 10, 15, or 20 replaced by the respective cap. These are sensitivity thresholds, not known upper bounds on valid answers. Each rule applies to all experimental groups and retains every respondent. For each cap, we recompute within-condition standard deviations, Hedges' $g$, the respondent bootstrap covariance, and the synthesis weights. These estimates describe differences in capped counts; their SD units differ from those of the uncapped estimates. Figure~\ref{fig:response_caps} shows all three caps alongside the uncapped contrasts, and Table~\ref{tab:pooled_sensitivity} summarizes the pooled results.
